## 1. What do the major classifieds and marketplace brand names mean, why were they chosen, and which naming types dominate?

### Takeaway
Most category leaders in Europe and the Arab world use a **plain word from the local language that means "market", "ads" or "auction"**, or a short, warm local phrase. Examples are Kleinanzeigen, Marktplaats, Tori, Haraj, OpenSooq/السوق المفتوح, Mazadat, Leboncoin, Sahibinden and Subito. **Coined or foreign names** are mostly used by mobile-first challengers and by companies that later expanded across countries: Wallapop, Vinted, Kijiji, Avito, OLX, Mercari, Carousell, dubizzle. Two forces shape these names. Local leaders won with descriptive or folksy names, and then moved even closer to the generic word (Kleinanzeigen dropped "eBay"). Groups that tried to replace loved local names with one global name have partly reversed course (dubizzle became OLX in 2015, then went back to dubizzle in Egypt and Lebanon).

| Eponym | Craigslist | Rare |

### Inferences
- In this category, **descriptive local-language names did not block market leadership**. Kleinanzeigen, Marktplaats, Haraj and OpenSooq lead their markets. But they win through network effects and being first, and the name gives little protection against copycats (see section 2 on the many "حراج" look-alike apps).
- The Kleinanzeigen case suggests that once a brand is dominant, **its generic-sounding name becomes the brand**. Dropping the "eBay" prefix cost little awareness (company-reported). For a newcomer, though, a generic name brings no such stored-up recognition.
- The dubizzle→OLX→dubizzle sequence and Vinted's merger of Kleiderkreisel into one brand point in opposite directions. Merging brands works when the brand is one regional product. Replacing a beloved local name with a global one can be undone. For a Syria-only app, the relevant lesson is to choose a name that can work if the app later expands regionally, without having to rename.
- Several mobile-first winners renamed early (Fleapster→Wallapop, sf-events→Craigslist, Mano Drabužiai→Vinted). Renaming before scale is cheap. After scale it costs a multi-year migration, as the Kleinanzeigen domain transition and the "dubizzle EG OLX" bridge titles show.

## 2. Do descriptive names help early discovery but hurt distinctiveness and trademarkability? How are generic words like سوق treated?

### Takeaway
Trademark law and naming research agree. **Generic words ("souq", "haraj", "market", "classifieds") can never be owned.** Descriptive names can be registered only after they acquire distinctiveness through use. Suggestive, arbitrary and invented names are protectable from day one. Real words, including irrelevant ones, are easier to recall than invented non-words, and a relevant name can "borrow" meaning when the budget is small. Invented names give more flexibility but need more spending and repeated exposure. App-store evidence shows that branded searches carry about half of App Store traffic and convert far better than generic searches. So in the long run a distinctive, searchable brand beats owning a generic word.

### Cited Findings
- **The distinctiveness spectrum** (from *Abercrombie & Fitch Co. v. Hunting World*, 1976), from weakest to strongest:
  - **Generic** (the common name of the goods or service): never protectable.
  - **Descriptive** (e.g. 5 MINUTE GLUE): registrable only with proof of acquired distinctiveness, or "secondary meaning".
  - **Suggestive** (Jaguar for cars): inherently distinctive.
  - **Arbitrary** (Apple for computers).
  - **Fanciful/invented** (Kodak, Xerox): strongest.

  Classification depends on the goods and services. "Apple" would be generic for a fruit company. — [UpCounsel](https://upcounsel.com/trademark-distinctiveness); [Trama TM](https://www.tramatm.com/trademark-questions-and-answers/specifics-of-us-trademarks/what-spectrum-does-the-uspto-use-to-evaluate-distinctiveness); [Dennemeyer](https://dennemeyer.com/ip-blog/news/twelve-common-misconceptions-about-us-trade-marks); [Carter Ledyard & Milburn PDF](https://www.cll.com/media/publication/515_Article-Milov-BrightIdeas-2021vol30no1.pdf)
- A common way to overcome a descriptiveness refusal is to argue the mark is suggestive ("Fast" is descriptive for cars, "Jaguar" is suggestive), or that it has a further layer of meaning such as a pun or incongruity. — [Trama TM, descriptiveness refusals](https://www.tramatm.com/en/trademark-questions-and-answers/office-actions/what-does-it-mean-when-my-trademark-is-refused-due-to-mere-descriptiveness)
- **Booking.com (US Supreme Court, 30 June 2020, 8–1):** the Court rejected the rule that "generic.com" is always generic. The test is whether consumers see the term as the name of a class of goods or as a source identifier. The registration **gives no monopoly over the word "booking"**, which stays generic for travel firms. Justice Breyer dissented, arguing that the term only describes the type of business. — [Nixon Peabody](https://nixonpeabody.com/insights/alerts/2020/07/01/uspto-v-booking); [Fish & Richardson](https://www.fr.com/insights/thought-leadership/blogs/supreme-court-finds-booking-com-protectable-rejects-sweeping-rule-denying-protection-to-generic-com-marks/); [Hunton](https://huntonak.com/hunton-retail-law-resource/consumer-perception-is-key-to-registration-of-generic-com-marks)
- The Benelux office (BOIP) refuses even non-dictionary abbreviations if consumers will read them as descriptive (example: "medi" for medical). — [BOIP](https://boip.int/en/node/4160). Other offices refuse common descriptive words because they "cannot be exclusively attributed to one particular source". — [Mondaq (Laos practice)](https://www.mondaq.com/southafrica/trademark/1309442/avoiding-descriptiveness-refusal-in-laos-key-considerations-for-trademark-owners)
- **UAE (Federal Decree-Law No. 36 of 2021, in force 2 January 2022):** Article 3 lists absolute grounds for refusal, including marks that **lack distinctive character or consist only of a generic name** for the goods or services. — [Kayrouz & Associates guide](https://www.kayrouzandassociates.com/insights/trademark-registration-uae-2026-guide); [official text (government.ae PDF)](https://government.ae/-/media/Documents-2022/Trademarks-law-no-36--of-2021-on-trademarks.pdf)
- **Syria:** trademarks are governed by **Law No. 8 of 2007** (in force 12 April 2007, implementing regulations 15 April 2007), as amended by Legislative Decree No. 47 of 2009 (protection of well-known marks, including unregistered ones). Syria is a Paris Convention member and uses Nice classification (11th edition). The Property Protection Office can require amendments to avoid confusion, with appeal to court within 30 days. — [AGIP Syria country page](https://www.agip.com/Agip-Country-Service/en/160/T); [AGIP news on the 2009 amendment](https://www.agip.com/News/en/news/1543); [WIPO Lex: Syria](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/fr/legislation/details/10781); [UNESCWA SME portal](https://smeportal.unescwa.org/node/674)
- **Generic Arabic words in app stores in practice:** searching for "Haraj" turns up many unrelated apps using the generic word حراج. Examples include **"حراج سوريا الرسمي" ("Haraj Syria Official", a Syrian app from a different developer)**, "حراج اكشن", "رواد حراج", "haraj1" and "حراج ٢٤". Users are told to check the developer name. — [App Store: حراج سوريا الرسمي](https://apps.apple.com/tn/app/6740450099); [App Store: حراج اكشن](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id6479557625); [MWM: haraj1](https://mwm.ai/apps/haraj1/6743768669)
- **Recall research:** Lerman & Garbarino (2002) compared word and non-word brand names. **Real-word names were recalled far more often (68.8% vs. 38.1%)**, and "irrelevant" real words were recalled about as well as relevant ones. These figures come from a secondary summary. — [Operative Words summary](https://www.operativewords.com/blog/2009/04/real-words-make-better-brand-names.html)
- **Kohli, Harich & Leuthesser (2005),** "Creating brand identity: a study of evaluation of new brand names", *J. Business Research* 58(11):1506–1515. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v58y2005i11p1506-1515.html). A secondary source quotes the paper: if flexibility and adaptability are priorities, "a non-meaningful name is attractive", while a relevant name "can perform better with smaller budgets because you can borrow existing meaning". — [Kolenda guide](https://www.kolenda.io/guides/brand-names) (not checked against the original)
- **Robertson (1989),** "Strategically Desirable Brand Name Characteristics", *J. Consumer Marketing* 6(4):61–71. A good name is simple, distinctive, meaningful and emotional. It uses sound devices like alliteration, **suggests the product class, and is legally protectable**. "A well-planned brand name will require less marketing money to achieve recall and image targets." — [Emerald](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/EUM0000000002563/full/html)
- A VUB research project page reports that **distinctive brand names are better remembered** overall. — [VUB research portal](https://researchportal.vub.be/en/projects/a-quest-for-the-perfect-brand-name-the-role-of-physical-brand-nam/)
- **Naming agency view (Igor):** names fall into Functional (descriptive), Invented, Experiential and Evocative types. Functional names are "the lowest common denominator", and evocative names rank highest for engagement. For search portals, Igor rates descriptive names like LookSmart and Infoseek low and Yahoo! high. — [Igor taxonomy](https://igorinternational.com/process/naming-company-names-taxonomy.php); [Igor web-portal taxonomy](https://igorinternational.com/process/web-portal-name-taxonomy.php). A counterpoint: invented and evocative names are so common that "the impact is dulled" ("Explorer and Safari are browsers — and SUVs"). — [Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/blog/218458)
- **App-store discovery (AppTweak, US App Store):** brand names make up only about 24% of keywords but drive **about 49% of App Store search traffic**. Over 97% of high-volume keywords (popularity > 75) are brand names. An app converts about 85% of searches for its own brand, vs. about 25% for the top app on a generic keyword. Generic searches are still more than half of traffic. These are modelled vendor estimates. — [AppTweak: brand vs generic](https://www.apptweak.com/en/aso-blog/brand-vs-generic-breakdown-in-app-store-search)

### Inferences
- A name built only from **سوق / souq / sooq / حراج / مزاد / إعلانات / "market" / "sale"** would be generic or descriptive for classifieds services almost everywhere. It could not be enforced against copycats, as the حراج clones show, and would be hard to register in Syria, the UAE or elsewhere without years of use. A compound mark (generic word plus a distinctive element, e.g. "Open"+"Sooq") can be registered as a whole, but the generic part stays free for competitors to use.
- The app-store evidence suggests the early discovery benefit of a descriptive name is small. Generic keywords can be targeted through the **subtitle/descriptor and short description** (section 5) rather than the brand name. The large gains come from branded searches, which only a distinctive name can own.
- The research suggests a middle path: **a real word, or a suggestive and easy-to-say name** (Leboncoin, Sahibinden, Kijiji, Careem). This gets the recall advantage of real words (Lerman & Garbarino) and a cheaper meaning transfer (Kohli et al.), while staying distinctive enough to protect (suggestive or arbitrary).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the text of Syrian Law No. 8/2007 on descriptive or generic signs (absolute grounds). Its Arabic text on WIPO Lex should be checked, or a Syrian IP attorney consulted.
- No decision or case found on "souq/souk" as a trademark (in Arabic or Latin script) in any GCC office, EUIPO or WIPO.
- No study found that measures directly how a descriptive vs. coined *app* name affects early organic installs. The evidence is indirect.

## 3. What linguistic and phonetic properties make names memorable?

### Takeaway
Memorable, well-liked names are **easy to pronounce on first sight (fluent)**, **short to medium in length** (roughly 2–3 syllables, and no more than 4 syllables internationally), often use **sound repetition** (Kijiji, Coca-Cola), and have **sounds that fit the product's character**. They must pass the **"radio/phone test"**: hear it once, spell it correctly. A 2026 study adds nuance: longer non-word names can be *recognised* better, while shorter names are *liked* better.

### Cited Findings
- **Sound repetition:** Argo, Popa & Smith (2010), "The Sound of Brands", *Journal of Marketing* 74(4):97–109. In tests of identical ice cream, the sample with a repetitive-sounding name was chosen more often. Names with phonetic repetition **were rated more favourably when spoken aloud**, but not when read silently. The effect held for a dessert, cat litter, hand sanitiser and a mobile phone. Too much repetition backfires: "ranthfanth" scored worse than "rantifanti". — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/10/101018092237.htm); [FoodNavigator](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2010/10/21/Repetitive-sounds-could-hold-secret-of-successful-brands/); [EconBiz record](https://econbiz.de/Record/the-sound-of-brands-argo-jennifer/10008826528)
- **Sound symbolism (vowels and consonants):** Klink (2000), "Creating Brand Names With Meaning: The Use of Sound Symbolism", *Marketing Letters* 11(1):5–20. Names with **front vowels** (i, e) seemed smaller, lighter, faster, friendlier and prettier. **Back vowels** (o, u) seemed larger and heavier. **Fricatives** (f, s, z) seemed smaller, faster and more feminine than **stops** (p, t, k). — [ProQuest](https://www.proquest.com/docview/204474588); [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5318481)
- Lowrey & Shrum (2007), "Phonetic Symbolism and Brand Name Preference", *J. Consumer Research* 34(3):406–414. Names were preferred when the vowel's connotation **fit a positive attribute of the category** (front vowels for convertibles and knives, back vowels for SUVs and hammers). Names with negative-sounding vowels were disliked in every category. — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/09/070912130815.htm); [Scinapse](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2124861461)
- **Pronunciation fluency:** Erz (Copenhagen Business School, EMAC 2015) ran three lab experiments on recall and recognition of easy- vs. hard-to-pronounce non-word names. — [CBS Research](https://research.cbs.dk/en/publications/can-you-say-my-name-effects-of-phonological-fluency-on-memory-of-/). The EU-funded FLUENTBRAND project tested 30 non-word names. Eye-tracking showed **less effort for easy-to-pronounce names**, and participants leaned toward them. — [CORDIS](https://cordis.europa.eu/article/id/175272-whats-in-a-brand-name)
- **Length:**
  - A June 2026 VUB policy brief (Zee, Willems, Brengman, Desmet) says the "two-syllable rule" oversimplifies. **Recognition improved as non-word names got longer**, while for liking, about 3–4 phonemes was the better benchmark. — [VUB SMIT Policy Brief 94](https://smit.research.vub.be/en/policy-brief-94-rightsizing-brand-names-the-effects-of-brand-name-length-on-brand-name-attitude-and)
  - A 2015 *J. Product & Brand Management* study (English versions of Chinese car brands) found shorter and semantically relevant names were seen as more memorable, and that pronounceability drove preference. — [Emerald](https://www.emeraldinsight.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/JPBM-02-2014-0501/full/html)
  - Lausanne/IMD research (2002): international names work better when easy to spell and pronounce. **Avoid names longer than 4 syllables.** — [ScienceSays summary](https://app.sciencesays.com/p/effective-international-brand-names)
- **Cross-language pronounceability by design:** Catchword chose Kijiji partly because its "repetitive, consonant-vowel structure is easy to pronounce around the globe", after checking hundreds of candidates in 12 languages. — [Catchword](https://catchwordbranding.com/portfolio/kijiji/)
- **Radio/phone test:** the person who hears the name on the radio should "know how to spell it in order to find it online or to recommend the brand". — [Namecheap](https://www.namecheap.com/blog/domain-name-radio-test/). One practical version: say the name on a phone call and see if the listener spells it back unaided, then test 3–5 candidates with target users for "confusion and cringe, not applause". — [eChai Ventures](https://echai.ventures/startingup/naming-domains/how-do-i-test-a-startup-name-on-real-people-before-i-commit-to-it). A modern variant is whether a voice assistant gets it right ("Ask Siri"). — [DomainInvesting](https://domaininvesting.com/does-it-pass-the-radio-test-ask-siri/). Agencies run formal consumer tests. — [Catchword testing tips](https://catchwordbranding.com/catchthis/tips-for-testing-a-new-company-name-with-consumers/)
- Robertson (1989): use sound devices such as **alliteration and consonance**, plus meaningful morphemes. — [Emerald](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/EUM0000000002563/full/html)

### Inferences
- For a Syrian app spread by **word of mouth** (WhatsApp voice notes, phone calls, conversations at the souq), the Argo et al. finding that matters is that the effect appears when the name is **spoken**. A name with light repetition or rhythm (CV-CV patterns such as Kijiji or Jiji) is an evidence-based choice.
- Front vowels (i, e) suggest small, quick and friendly, which fits "quick, easy, peer-to-peer selling". Back vowels suggest big and solid, which fits trust and security. This is a tie-breaker, not a primary criterion.
- The radio test has to be run **twice**: Arabic speakers hearing it and writing it in Arabic script, and Arabic or English speakers hearing it and typing it in Latin letters (section 4).

### Gaps
- I could not access full texts of these papers (sample sizes, effect sizes). The numbers above come from abstracts and press summaries.
- No research found on sound symbolism or fluency **for Arabic speakers or Arabic phonology** specifically. One Farsi study exists ([Alzahra BMR](https://bmr.alzahra.ac.ir/article_2852.html?lang=en)) but was not reviewed.

## 4. How have Arab brands handled dual names, and what are the transliteration, Arabizi and RTL pitfalls?

### Takeaway
Successful Arab consumer brands mostly **start from a short, positive Arabic word and pick one fixed Latin spelling**, sometimes deliberately unusual: Careem (كريم, "generous", K changed to C), Talabat (طلبات, "orders"), Anghami (أنغامي, "my tunes"), Mrsool (مرسول), Noon (نون), Jahez (جاهز), Tamara (تمارا, "date"). Or they choose an **English or coined name that writes cleanly in Arabic**: 4Sale/فورسيل, Tabby/تابي, dubizzle/دوبيزل. Problems arise from **sounds with no equivalent in the other script**:
- Arabic ع ح خ ق غ ض ط ص ظ have no Latin letter, which is why Arabizi uses digits like 3, 7 and 2.
- English P, V and hard G have no Arabic letter.
- Syrian dialect adds its own variation: Damascene speakers often pronounce ق as a glottal stop.

A name that spells one way in both scripts avoids all of these.

### Cited Findings

**Dual-name precedents**
- **Careem / كريم:** the founders "took the Arabic word for generous, *kareem*, and changed the K to a C as a play on words". The idea of being generous to customers, "captains" (drivers) and colleagues is tied to the brand's values. Founded in Dubai in 2012 and bought by Uber for about $3bn in 2020 (McKinsey's figure). — [The National](https://thenationalnews.com/business/journey-driven-by-generosity-1.292724); [McKinsey interview](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/careems-co-founders-on-finding-purpose-building-a-unicorn); [Arabian Business](https://www.arabianbusiness.com/in-driving-seat-careem-595522.html)
- **Talabat / طلبات** = "orders" or "requests". Founded in Kuwait in 2004 by students who modelled it on Egypt's **Otlob** (Arabic for "order!"). — [Wikipedia: Talabat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talabat); [Rasmal](https://www.rasmal.com/talabat/)
- **Anghami / أنغامي** = "My Tunes". Launched in 2012. — [AGBI](https://www.agbi.com/articles/make-some-noise-for-anghamis-elie-habib/)
- **Mrsool / مرسول:** the CTO says the name means "to send". The Latin spelling **drops a vowel** (Mrsool rather than Marsool). Founded in 2015 in Riyadh. — [HERE Technologies blog](https://www.here.com/learn/blog/mrsool-super-app); [Wikipedia: Mrsool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mrsool)
- **Jahez:** founded in 2016 by Ghassab Al-Mandil. — [Wamda 2020](https://www.wamda.com/en/2020/06/jahez-raises-36-million-series). (جاهز = "ready" is my gloss. No company source.)
- **Tamara:** the name means "date (fruit)" in Arabic and carries associations of sweetness and generosity. No company rationale found. — [Arabikey](https://arabikey.com/arabic-name/tamara/)
- **Tabby:** founded in 2019 by Hosam Arab and Daniil Barkalov. No source on the name. — [TechCrunch 2021](https://techcrunch.com/2021/08/04/dubai-based-buy-now-pay-later-platform-tabby-raises-50m-at-300m-valuation)
- **Transliterate or translate:** the same Arabic word *Itihad* ("union") became **Etihad** Airways (transliterated) and **Union** Bank (translated), which shows the two routes inside one market. — [The National: "Transliteration of names from Arabic is full of challenges"](https://thenational-the-national-prod.cdn.arcpublishing.com/opinion/comment/transliteration-of-names-from-arabic-is-full-of-challenges-1.304106)
- A 2014 AUS master's thesis on brand names in Dubai, Sharjah and Saudi Arabia found **transliteration the most used method** (flexible, cheap, legally required in some cases), but noted that "semantic loss for non-Arabic brand receivers is inevitable". — [AUS DSpace](https://dspace.aus.edu/xmlui/handle/11073/7519)
- FutureBrand's (2009) "Arabization" deck makes several points. Phonetic transliteration dominates: the brand keeps its sound, not its meaning (e.g. **Nakheel**, "many palms"). There is a spectrum of dual-language identities, from separate names to bilingual pairs to calligraphic or simultaneous lockups. Because Arabic has no "P", Pepsi is written "Bebsi". Words with ع (ʿayn) are "often avoided" because English speakers struggle with it. — [SlideShare: Arabization](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/arabization/16610615)
- A Qatar agency guide (April 2026) proposes filters applied in order:
  1. Pronounceable by both audiences.
  2. **Means nothing wrong** in either language. Check with native speakers (MSA, dialect, English slang), not machine translation.
  3. **Clears trademark search in both Arabic and Latin script.**

  It describes three models: an existing Arabic word, an English or coined name that transliterates cleanly, or a paired bilingual name. **Paired names cost more** (two trademarks, two domains). It warns that English names containing **P, V or a hard G** often fail in Arabic. — [Bilingual brand naming guide](https://freezil-website-wjfnk.kinsta.page/blog/bilingual-brand-naming-guide)

**Script and sound pitfalls**
- Arabic's script, its unwritten short vowels, colonial-language influence and dialects all produce several Latin spellings of one word (e.g. Mohammed / Muhammad). — [Sayari](https://learn.sayari.com/?p=4462)
- **Arabizi** (the Arabic chat alphabet) uses digits for missing sounds:

  | Digit | Arabic letter |
  |---|---|
  | 2 | ء/أ (hamza) |
  | 3 | ع |
  | 3' | غ |
  | 5 | خ |
  | 6 | ط |
  | 7 | ح |
  | 8 | ق (some writers) |
  | 9 | ص |

  It is **not standardised** and varies by person and dialect. — [Arabic for Nerds](https://arabic-for-nerds.com/tools/arabizi-arabic-chat-alphabet/); [GulfArabic.com](https://gulfarabic.com/a12_arabiizi_chat_arabic.php); [Rosetta Stone blog](https://blog.rosettastone.com/arabizi-the-chat-alphabet-for-informal-arabic-dialects/)
- **Hatla2ee** is an example of a big brand with an Arabizi digit in its Latin name. It became Egypt's leading car platform (2M+ monthly visitors) and was acquired. — [Enterprise AM](https://enterpriseam.com/egypt/2025/02/19/dubais-dubizzle-acquires-hatla2ee/)
- **P and V:** Arabic speakers often merge p/b and v/f, because "neither 'p' nor 'v' exists in the language". — [ProZ forum](https://lit.proz.com/forum/arabic/73236-arabic_speakers_speaking_english_common_mistakes.html). A Syrian example: a Damascus airport sign spelled "Prestige" as **"Brestige"** (2004). — [Natasha Tynes blog](https://www.natashatynes.com/blogsarchive/2004/11/13/spotted-at-the-damascus-airport)
- **Syrian dialect and ق:** urban Damascene Arabic usually pronounces ق (qāf) as a **glottal stop** (e.g. "alb" for qalb, "heart"). Everyday words tend to drop the q, while words borrowed from formal Arabic keep it. — [Strommen tutors](https://strommeninc.com/syrian-arabic-tutors/); [Team Maha](https://teammaha.com/2023/03/consonants/). Speakers in Aleppo, and Druze and Alawite speakers, often keep the uvular /q/, and **not every Syrian variety replaces it**. — [Kentalis handout (PDF)](https://kentalis.nl/file-download/download/public/898); [Adrosverse](https://www.adrosverse.com/?p=14773)

**RTL and logo**
- MBZUAI's 2026 brand guidelines provide **separate bilingual, English-only and Arabic-only logos**. In the bilingual lockup the English side is left-aligned and the Arabic side right-aligned, and the logo version should "imitate the language/s present on the asset". — [MBZUAI Brand Guidelines, March 2026 (PDF)](https://staticcdn.mbzuai.ac.ae/mbzuaiwpprd01/2026/04/MBZUAI_BRAND-GUIDELINES_MARCH_2026_V1.pdf)
- In Arabic layouts the logo moves to the top-right and navigation is reversed. — [Creative4All](https://iraq.creative4all.com/en/blog/bilingual-website-design/). Icons that contain English characters should not be mirrored, but should be localised. — [Greenpeace RTL principles (PDF)](https://jira.greenpeace.org/secure/attachment/27098/Right-to-Left%20_%20Overall%20design%20principles.pdf). A Latin brand name inside Arabic text is mixed-direction ("bidi") and can display out of order, so it should be kept as an isolated element. — [Website Spec: RTL support](https://specification.website/spec/i18n/rtl-support/)

### Inferences
- Rules that follow from this evidence for a Syrian bilingual name:
  1. **The Arabic form should come first and be a real, positive or neutral Syrian or Levantine word or phrase** (or a short coinage). The Latin form should be **one obvious spelling**.
  2. **Avoid ع ح خ غ ق ض ظ ط ص in the core name.** Each forces a digit or a spelling choice in Latin script (e.g. 7araj/Haraj/Harraj), and ق is pronounced two ways inside Syria itself.
  3. **Avoid Latin P, V and hard G** if the Arabic form must be pronounced natively.
  4. **Avoid hamza and long-vowel spelling choices** (sooq/souq/suq/souk; ee/i/y). Each variant leaks searches and typed domains.
  5. Prefer letters that map one-to-one: b, t, d, r, z, s, sh, f, k, l, m, n, h, w, y, plus the vowels a, i, u.
- The Careem and Mrsool examples show that a **distinctive Latin spelling of an Arabic word** (C for K, dropped vowel) can make the Latin form ownable even when the Arabic word is common. The cost is that people guessing from the sound will mistype it, so the brand should register the main misspellings and defensive domains.
- Arabizi digits (Hatla2ee) can work in a young, informal market. But they rule out a clean Latin trademark, look odd to non-Arab users, and voice assistants handle them poorly. They suit a sub-brand or slogan better than the main mark.
- Logo: plan **three lockups** (Arabic-only, Latin-only, bilingual). The Arabic wordmark should be designed, not just typed in a font, with similar visual weight to the Latin one. The app icon should probably carry a script-neutral symbol or a single letter, as in Kleinanzeigen's "K" and dubizzle's flame.

### Gaps
- Spelling variants of "souq" (souq/souk/suq/sooq): planned search not run (budget exhausted).
- No company sources on how Noon, Jahez, Tabby, Tamara, HungerStation or Fetchr chose their names.
- No research found on how Syrian users specifically write brand names in Latin script, or on Syrian-dialect Arabizi conventions (e.g. whether 8 or 2 is used for ق).

## 5. App store optimisation: how much does the app name/title field matter, and is "Brand – descriptor" the norm?

### Takeaway
On both stores the **30-character title is the most heavily weighted text field for ranking**. Google Play cut it from 50 to 30 characters in 2021 and bans keyword stuffing such as "best", "#1" and "free". Market leaders follow a consistent pattern: **short brand plus the brand in the other script, or a 1–2 word generic descriptor**. Examples: "حراج - Haraj", "السوق المفتوح - OpenSooq", "Mazadat - مزادات", "4SaLe [ فورسيل ]", "dubizzle EG OLX". So a short brand name (ideally 12 characters or fewer per script) leaves room for the second script or a keyword.

### Cited Findings
- **Google Play policy:** the app title must be **30 characters or less**. No emojis, emoticons or repeated special characters. No ALL CAPS unless that is the brand. **No text that indicates store performance or ranking, price or promotion, or links to Google Play programs** in the title, icon or developer name. Misleading, irrelevant or excessive metadata and spammy keywords are not allowed. — [Google Play Console Help: metadata](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9898842?hl=en)
- The limit dropped from 50 to 30 characters under a policy effective **29 September 2021**. — [AppTweak](https://www.apptweak.com/en/aso-blog/how-to-shorten-your-app-name-on-the-play-store). Banned examples reported at the time include "top", "#1", "best", "free", "no ads", "download now". — [Android Central](https://androidcentral.com/google-clamping-down-misleading-play-store-app-listings); [Moburst](https://www.moburst.com/blog/google-play-policy-changes-2021/); [AppTweak: metadata policy](https://www.apptweak.com/en/aso-blog/how-to-prepare-for-new-google-metadata-policy-changes)
- **How much the title weighs:** AppFollow calls it the highest-weighted ranking signal on iOS and Google Play, often "2–3×" the impact of other fields (a vendor estimate; Google publishes no weights). — [AppFollow](https://appfollow.io/blog/app-store-optimization-title). Sonar ranks the title highest, then the short description, then the long description. — [Sonar Android ASO guide](https://trysonar.app/blog/android-aso-guide.md). Google's algorithm reads the title, short description and full description together. — [Gummicube](https://gummicube.com/how-aso-works-google)
- Well-known brands usually pair their name with **1–2 generic keywords**. Keyword stuffing "will look weird, decrease your conversion rate, and hurt rankings". Use ":" or "&" to save characters. — [AppRadar: app title](https://appradar.com/academy/app-title)
- **Apple App Store:** app name 30 characters, **subtitle 30 characters** (indexed for search), and a hidden keyword field of 100 (possibly bytes, which matters for Arabic text). Each locale has its own keyword bank. Don't repeat words already in the name or subtitle. — [Sonar: iOS title length](https://trysonar.app/blog/ios-app-title-length); [AppLaunchFlow](https://www.applaunchflow.com/blog/app-store-metadata-character-limits-2026); [Adalo](https://studio.adalo.com/blog/app-store-keywords-field)
- **Store titles of regional competitors (2026 snapshots via search):**
  - Haraj: Google Play **"حراج - Haraj"** (10M+ installs, about 4.77★ from about 620k ratings). iOS **"حراج"** with subtitle **"بيع و اشتري | جديد أو مستعمل"** ("buy & sell | new or used"). — [AppBrain: Haraj](https://www.appbrain.com/dev/Haraj/); [App Store: Haraj](https://apps.apple.com/app/id662557215)

  - **"Mazadat - مزادات"**. — [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id1537442198)
  - **"4SaLe [ فورسيل ]"**. — [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6478521238)

- **Branded vs. generic search:** branded keywords drive about 49% of App Store traffic, an app converts about 85% of searches for its own brand vs. about 25% for the top app on a generic term, and over 97% of high-volume keywords are brands. — [AppTweak](https://www.apptweak.com/en/aso-blog/brand-vs-generic-breakdown-in-app-store-search)
- In Israel's App Store, AppTweak found **English keyword volume was usually higher than the Hebrew translation**, and the top results for English and Hebrew queries were "totally different". So each language needs its own optimisation. — [AppTweak: localize ASO in Hebrew & Arabic](https://www.apptweak.com/en/aso-blog/why-you-should-localize-your-aso-in-hebrew-arabic). Vendor guides for Saudi Arabia say direct translations miss popular local terms, and keyword research should cover dialect and transliteration. — [AppDrift: Saudi Arabia ASO](https://appdrift.co/app-store-optimization/saudi-arabia/shopping); [Phiture: ASO in Arabic countries](https://phiture.com/asostack/aso-in-arabic-countries-for-non-native-speakers/)

### Inferences
- The regional norm is **"[Arabic brand] - [Latin brand]"** or the reverse, which makes the app findable in both scripts with no keyword stuffing. That favours a name that is **short in both scripts**. For example, "حراج - Haraj" is 12 characters and "السوق المفتوح - OpenSooq" is 24.
- Remaining space (the iOS subtitle, and the Google Play title plus short description) should carry the generic intent words Syrians search for, such as بيع وشراء, مستعمل, سيارات, عقارات and "buy & sell". The brand itself does not need to be descriptive to rank for these.
- A name that shares a generic word with existing apps (حراج, سوق, مزاد) will compete with dozens of near-identical titles in search results. That weakens the 85% own-brand conversion advantage, because users cannot tell which app is the real one.

### Gaps
- Google publishes no ranking weights. All weight claims are vendor estimates.
- No Syria-storefront keyword volume data found (e.g. Arabic vs. English vs. Arabizi searches for "سيارات مستعملة" / "used cars" / "sayarat"). Google Play availability and paid-app limits in Syria after sanctions relief were not researched here.

## 6. Naming for a market with a large diaspora searching in Latin script

### Takeaway
Many Syrians live abroad, and they are a likely second audience: senders of remittances, buyers for family, and returnees. **About 1.7 million refugees had returned by May 2026, and UNHCR expects about 1 million more in 2026.** Diaspora and returnee users often type in Latin script or English. That argues for a name with **one unambiguous Latin spelling that also reads naturally in Arabic**. I found no direct data on how the diaspora searches.

### Cited Findings
- UNHCR dashboard: **1,667,078 Syrian refugee returnees as of 28 May 2026**, registered and unregistered, figures subject to revision. — [UNHCR data portal: Syrian returnees](https://data.unhcr.org/es/country/syr/112)
- UNHCR expects about **1 million more returns in 2026**, which would bring the total to more than 4 million within two years (refugees plus internally displaced people). Returns come mainly from Turkey, Lebanon and Jordan. — [North Press Agency](https://npasyria.com/en/133544/); [UNHCR regional update, Apr–May 2026 (PDF)](https://www.unhcr.org/sites/default/files/docs/default/2026-07/unhcr-returns-to-syria-regional-update-April-May-2026_0.pdf)
- 196,000 refugees returned from Jordan between 8 December 2024 and the end of May 2026. — [Jordan Times](https://jordantimes.com/news/local/196000-syrian-refugees-returned-from-jordan-since-december-2024-unhcr)
- Regional players are entering Syria. Noon opened an official Damascus office in May 2026. — [Enab Baladi](https://english.enabbaladi.net/?p=94896)
- Indirect evidence on search language: Israeli users tend to search in English more than Hebrew ([AppTweak](https://www.apptweak.com/en/aso-blog/why-you-should-localize-your-aso-in-hebrew-arabic)). A UAE agency blog says UAE residents mostly search in English, while Saudi users search in Arabic or **transliterated terms**. That refers to web search, not app stores, and I am not fully sure this URL is the original source. — [Chain Reaction (UAE agency)](https://www.chainreaction.ae/?p=2999)
- Gumtree is an example of a name built for **expat communities**: one word that "linked the three communities" (Australians, New Zealanders and South Africans in London). — [Wikipedia: Gumtree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gumtree)

### Inferences
- Returnees from Turkey, Germany and Lebanon, along with those still abroad, will often meet the brand through **Latin-script channels**: Google search, app stores set to German, Turkish or English, Instagram handles, URLs. Inside Syria the Arabic form will dominate. **The name must survive both journeys**: hear it in Arabic and type it in Latin, or see it in Latin and say it in Arabic.
- A name with Arabizi digits or with ع/ح/خ/ق will be typed differently by diaspora users depending on their host-country language. For example, ش is written sh in English, ş in Turkish and sch in German, and خ is written kh or ch. **Turkish and German keyboards add more spelling variants.** Prefer letters that are spelled the same way in English, German and Turkish.
- In the store listings, include common Latin spellings of the brand, and "Syria / سوريا" plus city names, in the description and keyword fields for diaspora storefronts (Germany, Turkey, Sweden, Lebanon, Jordan, UAE).

### Gaps
- No data found on the total number of Syrians abroad in 2026, or by country. The UNHCR registered-refugee table in the regional update could not be extracted reliably.
- No studies found on the Latin-script, Arabizi or English search behaviour of Syrian diaspora or returnees in app stores or on Google.
- Could not research the availability of the Syrian domains .sy and the Arabic-script .سوريا (search budget exhausted). This belongs to the name-availability workstream.
